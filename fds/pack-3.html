3.1

Similarity, dissimilarity and proximity

Similarity is a numerical measure of how alike two data objects are: the value is higher when the objects are more alike and often falls in [0, 1]. Dissimilarity (for example a distance) is a numerical measure of how different two objects are: it is lower when the objects are more alike, its minimum is often 0, and the upper limit varies. Proximity refers to either a similarity or a dissimilarity.

Almost all of data mining and data science that groups, searches or classifies data (clustering, nearest-neighbour classification, outlier detection, recommendation, plagiarism detection, spell-checking) rests on a proximity measure, so choosing the right one for the attribute type is the main skill of this module.

  • A similarity can be turned into a dissimilarity with d = 1 − s (when s is in [0, 1]); a distance can be turned into a similarity with s = 1/(1 + d).
  • The right measure depends on the attribute type: nominal, binary (symmetric or asymmetric), ordinal, numeric (interval or ratio), text, or a mixture.
3.2

Why this comes after attribute types and descriptive statistics

Data objects are described by attributes (database rows are objects, columns are attributes). Types: nominal (names of things), binary (symmetric or asymmetric), ordinal (order but unknown spacing), interval (equal units, no true zero, such as °C) and ratio (true zero, such as kelvin or length). Attributes may also be discrete (finite or countably infinite values) or continuous (real values).

Statistical descriptions (mean, median, mode, quartiles, variance, boxplots) tell you the scale and spread of each attribute; that information is what is used to standardise attributes before a distance is computed.

3.4

Nominal attributes

A nominal attribute can take two or more states (red, yellow, blue, green). It generalises a binary attribute.

MethodFormulaIdea
Simple matchingd(i, j) = (p − m) / pm = number of attributes on which i and j match, p = total number of nominal attributes

Example: objects (red, round, small) and (red, square, small): m = 2, p = 3, so d = 1/3 and the similarity is 2/3.

3.5

Binary attributes: the contingency table

symmetric binary  d(i, j) = r + sq + r + s + tasymmetric binary  d(i, j) = r + sq + r + sJaccard  sim(i, j) = qq + r + s = 1 − d(i, j)

For two objects i and j described by p binary attributes, count the positions by their value pair. Let q = both 1, r = 1 in i and 0 in j, s = 0 in i and 1 in j, t = both 0; then p = q + r + s + t.

  • Symmetric binary (gender): both outcomes are equally important, so the 0–0 matches (t) count.
  • Asymmetric binary (a medical test: positive or negative): the outcomes are not equally important. By convention the more important, rarer outcome (HIV positive) is coded 1. Agreement on 0–0 says little, so t is ignored.
  • The Jaccard coefficient is the similarity for asymmetric binary attributes and is the same as "coherence". The simple matching coefficient is (q + t)/(q + r + s + t) = 1 − symmetric distance.
  • The Hamming distance between two binary vectors is the number of positions that differ, r + s.

3.7

Standardising numeric data

z-score  z = x − μσmean absolute deviation  sf = 1n( |x1f − mf| + |x2f − mf| + … + |xnf − mf| ),   zif = xif − mfsf

Attributes measured on different scales (age in years, salary in rupees) must be put on a common footing before a distance is computed, otherwise the larger scale dominates.

  • The z-score is the distance of the raw score from the population mean in units of the standard deviation; it is negative below the mean and positive above it.
  • Using the mean absolute deviation instead of σ is more robust, because deviations are not squared and an outlier is therefore damped less severely than by the standard deviation.
3.8

Minkowski distance and its special cases

d(i, j) = ( |xi1 − xj1|h + |xi2 − xj2|h + … + |xip − xjp|h )1/h

For two p-dimensional objects i = (xi1, …, xip) and j = (xj1, …, xjp) and an order h, the Minkowski distance (the L-h norm) is:

  • h = 1: Manhattan (city block, L1 norm). The Hamming distance of binary vectors is the special case.
  • h = 2: Euclidean (L2 norm).
  • h → ∞: supremum (L-max or L∞ norm): the maximum difference over any attribute.
3.8b

A distance that satisfies all three properties is a metric; Minkowski distance (h ≥ 1) is one.

3.12

Cosine similarity

cos(d1, d2) = d1 · d2||d1|| ||d2||

A document can be represented by thousands of attributes, each recording the frequency of one word or phrase (a term-frequency vector). Such vectors are long and sparse: most entries are 0. Euclidean distance between them mostly measures document length and the many 0–0 matches, so a different measure is used. Other vector objects are gene features in micro-arrays; applications are information retrieval, biological taxonomy and gene feature mapping.

  • d1 · d2 is the dot product and ||d|| the length of the vector, Σ dk2.
  • The value is the cosine of the angle between the vectors: 1 means the same direction (identical proportions), 0 means orthogonal (nothing in common), and it ignores vector length.
  • It is a similarity, not a metric. The cosine distance is 1 − cos.

d1 = (5, 0, 3, 0, 2, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0), d2 = (3, 0, 2, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1) (from the slides).

d1·d2 = 5·3 + 0·0 + 3·2 + 0·0 + 2·1 + 0·1 + 0·1 + 2·1 + 0·0 + 0·1 = 25.

3.14

Mahalanobis distance

dM(x, y) = (x − y)T S−1 (x − y)

Euclidean distance treats every attribute as independent and equally scaled. Mahalanobis distance accounts for the variance of each attribute and the correlation between attributes through the covariance matrix S. Points that lie along the main direction of the data cloud are close; points the same Euclidean distance away but across the cloud are far.

  • If S is the identity matrix, dM is the Euclidean distance. If S is diagonal, it is the Euclidean distance on attributes divided by their standard deviations (a standardised Euclidean distance).
  • It is scale-invariant (changing units changes nothing) and requires an invertible S, so attributes must not be perfectly correlated. Typical use: outlier detection in multivariate data.
3.15

Bhattacharyya distance

BC(p, q) = Σ pi qiDB(p, q) = −ln BC(p, q)

Used for probability distributions (and histograms). For discrete distributions p and q over the same categories, the Bhattacharyya coefficient measures their overlap, and the distance is its negative logarithm.

  • BC lies in [0, 1]: 1 for identical distributions, 0 when they do not overlap at all. DB is 0 for identical distributions and grows as they separate.
  • The Hellinger distance, 1 − BC, is closely related and is a true metric. DB does not satisfy the triangle inequality.
  • Applications: comparing colour histograms of images, feature selection and separability of classes in classification.
3.16

Bregman divergence

Dψ(x, y) = ψ(x) − ψ(y) − ⟨∇ψ(y), x − y⟩

History. Named after Lev M. Bregman, who introduced the concept in 1967. Bregman divergences are similar to metrics but satisfy neither the triangle inequality (ever) nor symmetry (in general). They satisfy a generalisation of the Pythagorean theorem, which lets many optimisation techniques be generalised to them, geometrically as generalisations of least squares. In machine learning they are used, for example, in the bi-tempered logistic loss, which performs better than softmax on noisy data.

Definition. Let ψ: Ω → R be (a) strictly convex, (b) continuously differentiable and (c) defined on a closed convex set Ω. The divergence is the difference between the value of ψ at x and the first-order Taylor expansion of ψ around y evaluated at x. It is a family of proximity functions that represent a loss or distortion: y is the original point, x a noisy approximation of it.

  • ∇ψ(y) is the gradient of ψ at y; x − y is the difference vector; ⟨·,·⟩ is the inner product (the dot product in Euclidean space).
  • For real numbers with ψ(t) = t²: D(x, y) = x² − y² − 2y(x − y) = (x − y)². With y = 1 this is 1 for x = 2 and 4 for x = 3.
3.17

Bregman divergences: examples and properties

Choosing the convex function ψ gives the familiar measures:

ψDivergence D_ψ(x, y)Name
ψ(x) = ‖x‖²‖x − y‖²Squared Euclidean distance
ψ(p) = Σ pj log pj (negative entropy)Σ pj log(pj / qj)Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
ψ(x) = −Σ log xjΣ ( xj/yj − log(xj/yj) − 1 )Itakura–Saito distance

Properties: non-negativity, D(x, y) ≥ 0, and = 0 only if x = y (from convexity); convex in the first argument, but not necessarily in the second; linearity in ψ; not a metric (no symmetry in general, no triangle inequality); a generalised law of cosines and Pythagorean theorem holds; the mean minimises the expected divergence (see next plate). KL divergence between two distributions of the same exponential family is a Bregman divergence.

3.18

Bregman information and the mean as best predictor

For squared loss the mean is the best constant predictor of a random variable: μ = argminc E[‖X − c‖2], and the minimum loss is the variance. The theorem generalises: for every Bregman divergence the mean minimises the expected divergence, μ = argminc E[Dψ(X, c)]. The minimum loss is called the Bregman information of X, Iψ(X) = E[Dψ(X, μ)].

  • ψ(x) = ‖x‖²: Bregman information = variance.
  • ψ = negative entropy: Bregman information = mutual information I(Z; Y).
  • ψ(x) = −Σ log xj with X uniform over the points: log of the ratio arithmetic mean / geometric mean.
  • This is why k-means-type clustering works for all Bregman divergences, not only for squared Euclidean distance.
3.19

Textual similarity: edit distance

The edit (Levenshtein) distance between two strings is the minimum number of insertions, deletions and substitutions (each cost 1) needed to turn one into the other. It is computed by dynamic programming.

D(i, j) = min D(i−1, j) + 1,   D(i, j−1) + 1,   D(i−1, j−1) + [ai ≠ bj]

Boundary: D(i, 0) = i and D(0, j) = j. The answer is the bottom-right cell. The table is filled row by row; tracing back from the corner shows the operations. Uses: spell-checking, DNA sequence alignment, fuzzy record matching.

3.20

Jaro and Jaro–Winkler similarity

simJ = 13( m|s1| + m|s2| + m − tm )simW = simJ + ℓ p (1 − simJ)

Designed for short strings such as names. Two characters match if they are equal and not farther apart than ⌊ max(|s1|, |s2|)/2 ⌋ − 1 positions. Let m be the number of matching characters and t half the number of matching characters that appear in a different order (transpositions).

  • simJ = 0 if m = 0. Jaro–Winkler boosts the score for strings with a common prefix: ℓ = length of the common prefix (at most 4), p = scaling factor, normally 0.1.
  • Result is in [0, 1]; 1 means identical strings. MARTHA and MARHTA: m = 6, t = 1, simJ = 0.944, simW = 0.961.
3.21

n-gram similarity of strings

Dice  2 |G1 ∩ G2||G1| + |G2|,   Jaccard  |G1 ∩ G2||G1 ∪ G2|

An n-gram is a continuous sequence of n items (characters, letters, phonemes or words) from a text. Strings are compared by the n-grams they share. For bigrams, night → ni, ig, gh, ht; nacht → na, ac, ch, ht.

  • Distance = 1 − similarity. Tolerant of typos and word-order changes; used in plagiarism detection and fuzzy search.
  • n = 1 unigram (single words), n = 2 bigram (pairs), n = 3 trigram (triples). In practice bigrams, trigrams and four-grams are used.
3.22

N-gram language models: the goal

A language model assigns a probability to a sequence of words; it answers "How likely is this sentence?". Goal: compute the probability of a sentence or sequence of words, P(W) = P(w1, w2, …, wn). Related task: the probability of an upcoming word, P(w4 | w1, w2, w3). A model that computes either of these is called a language model.

An N-gram language model predicts the probability of the next word from the previous (N − 1) words: unigram uses no history, bigram uses one previous word, trigram two. Example "How are you ?": unigram predicts ? with no history, bigram from "you", trigram from "are you".

  • Applications: search engines (suggesting the next words), speech recognition (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant), machine translation (Google Translate), chatbots and predictive text on smartphones ("I am the king of …" → suggestions).
  • It is based on probability: the phone suggests words that most often followed the typed words.
3.23

The chain rule and the Markov assumption

P(w1, w2, …, wn) = P(w1) P(w2 | w1) P(w3 | w1, w2) … P(wn | w1, …, wn−1) = Πi P(wi | w1 … wi−1)

From conditional probability, P(B | A) = P(A, B)/P(A), so P(A, B) = P(A),P(B | A), and in general:

  • Example: P(about fifteen minutes from) = P(about) × P(fifteen | about) × P(minutes | about fifteen) × P(from | about fifteen minutes).
  • Problem: long histories rarely occur in any corpus, so we might not have enough data to estimate them. A simplification is needed.
  • Markov assumption: use only the previous word, or a couple of previous words: P(office | about fifteen minutes from) ≈ P(office | from) or ≈ P(office | minutes from).
  • k-th order Markov model: P(w1 … wn) ≈ Πi P(wi | wi−k … wi−1). N-gram model = (N − 1)-order Markov model. Unigram: P(office). Bigram: P(office | from). Trigram: P(office | minutes from). It extends to 4-grams and 5-grams.
3.24

Estimating n-gram probabilities: MLE and markers

unigram  P(wi) = C(wi)Nbigram  P(wn | wn−1) = C(wn−1, wn)C(wn−1)trigram  P(wn | wn−2, wn−1) = C(wn−2, wn−1, wn)C(wn−2, wn−1)

Probabilities are estimated by counting in a corpus (a database of text) using the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE).

  • N = total number of tokens. Bigram count over unigram count.
  • Markers: <s> marks the start and </s> the end of a sentence; they capture sentence boundaries and word position. "I am Amit" becomes <s> I am Amit </s>. Bigrams: (<s>, I), (I, am), (am, Amit), (Amit, </s>). For trigrams the start is padded with two markers, <s> <s> I, to give context to the first word.
3.25

Problems of MLE: underflow and zero probability

Two practical problems arise.

  • Underflow: multiplying many probabilities gives a tiny number. Solution: add logarithms, log(p₁ × p₂ × p₃) = log p₁ + log p₂ + log p₃.
  • Zero probability: a bigram never seen in the corpus has probability 0 and makes the whole sentence probability 0. Solution: smoothing; the course uses Laplace (add-one) smoothing.
  • Laplace smoothing, as done in class: (1) count the unique words in the corpus (including <s> and </s>); (2) exclude <s> from the count, giving V; (3) for every probability add 1 to the numerator and V to the denominator: P = (C(wi-1, wi) + 1)/(C(wi-1) + V).
  • Limitation of n-gram models: language has long-distance dependencies, so an n-gram model is not sufficient. Example: "The computer which I had just put into the machine room on the fifth floor crashed": the verb must agree with "computer", many words earlier.

Q1

Define similarity, dissimilarity and proximity. How are they related?

Show model answer

Similarity is a numerical measure of how alike two objects are; it is higher for more alike objects and often lies in [0, 1]. Dissimilarity (for example distance) measures how different they are; it is lower for more alike objects, its minimum is often 0 and its upper limit varies. Proximity refers to either of them.

  • Conversion: d = 1 − s when s ∈ [0, 1], or s = 1/(1 + d).
  • Example: two students with the same marks have similarity 1 and distance 0.

Q3

How is the proximity of nominal attributes measured? Give two methods.

Show model answer

Method 1, simple matching: d(i, j) = (p − m)/p where m is the number of attributes on which the two objects have the same state and p the total number of attributes. Method 2, binary encoding: create a new binary attribute for each of the M nominal states (one-hot) and use the binary measures.

Q5

Why is the 0–0 match ignored for asymmetric binary attributes? What is the Jaccard coefficient?

Show model answer

When most attributes are rare (for example symptoms or purchased items), two patients who both lack almost everything are not meaningfully alike; counting those matches would make every pair look similar. The Jaccard coefficient q/(q + r + s) is the similarity that counts only the 1–1 matches against all positions where at least one object has a 1. It is the same as "coherence" and equals 1 − d for the asymmetric distance.

Q6

Why standardise numeric attributes? Compare the z-score with the mean-absolute-deviation method.

Show model answer

If attributes use different units or ranges (age 0–100, salary in lakhs), the one with the larger numbers dominates the distance. Standardising gives each equal weight.

  • z-score: z = (x − μ)/σ; negative below the mean, positive above it, in standard-deviation units.
  • Mean absolute deviation: sf = 1nΣ |xif − mf|, zif = (xif − mf)/sf.
  • The MAD version is more robust to outliers, since deviations are not squared, so an outlier inflates sf less than it inflates σ.
Q7

Define Minkowski distance and state its special cases.

Show model answer

For p-dimensional objects i and j, d(i, j) = (Σk |xik − xjk|h)1/h with order h (the L-h norm).

  • h = 1: Manhattan (city block), sum of absolute differences. Hamming distance for binary vectors is a special case.
  • h = 2: Euclidean, the straight-line distance.
  • h → ∞: supremum (L-max), the largest single-attribute difference.
  • Larger h gives more weight to the biggest difference; for the same pair, L∞ ≤ L2 ≤ L1.
Q8

What is a metric? Which of the measures in this module are metrics?

Show model answer

A distance is a metric if it has (1) positive definiteness: d(i, j) > 0 for i ≠ j and d(i, i) = 0; (2) symmetry: d(i, j) = d(j, i); (3) triangle inequality: d(i, j) ≤ d(i, k) + d(k, j).

Q9

How are ordinal attributes handled in a dissimilarity computation?

Show model answer

Treat them like interval-scaled attributes after three steps: (1) replace each value by its rank rif ∈ {1, …, Mf}; (2) map the rank onto [0, 1], zif = (rif − 1)/(Mf − 1), so each attribute has equal weight; (3) compute the dissimilarity with an interval-scaled measure such as |zi − zj| or Euclidean distance. Example with fair < good < excellent: z = 0, 0.5, 1.

Q10

Explain how dissimilarity is computed for attributes of mixed type.

Show model answer

Compute a dissimilarity per attribute on a common [0, 1] scale and take a weighted average: d(i, j) = Σf δij(f) dij(f) / Σf δij(f). For binary or nominal attributes d = 0 if the values are equal, else 1; for numeric attributes d = |xif − xjf|/(maxf − minf); for ordinal attributes use the normalised rank z as numeric. δ = 0 if a value is missing (or for asymmetric attributes when both values are 0), otherwise 1.

Q11

What is cosine similarity? Why is it used for documents?

Show model answer

For term-frequency vectors d1 and d2, cos(d1, d2) = (d1 · d2)/(‖d1‖ ‖d2‖), the cosine of the angle between them.

  • Documents have thousands of attributes and most entries are 0 (sparse). Euclidean distance is dominated by length and the many 0–0 matches.
  • Cosine similarity ignores length: a document and the same document repeated twice have cosine 1.
  • Range: 1 identical direction, 0 nothing in common (non-negative counts).
  • Applications: information retrieval, biological taxonomy, gene-feature mapping.
Q12

What is Mahalanobis distance and when is it better than Euclidean distance?

Show model answer

dM(x, y) = (x − y)T S−1 (x − y) where S is the covariance matrix.

  • It rescales each attribute by its variance and accounts for correlation between attributes, so it is scale-invariant.
  • With S = I it equals Euclidean distance; with S diagonal it is standardised Euclidean distance.
  • Better when attributes are correlated or on different scales: two points at the same Euclidean distance from the mean can have very different Mahalanobis distances (a point off the trend is farther). Used for outlier detection.
  • Needs an invertible S.
Q13

Define the Bhattacharyya coefficient and distance.

Show model answer

For discrete distributions p and q, BC = Σi pi qi measures the overlap (1 for identical, 0 for disjoint) and DB = −ln BC is the distance (0 for identical). It compares distributions or histograms; the related Hellinger distance 1 − BC is a metric while DB is not (the triangle inequality fails).

Q14

Define Bregman divergence. What conditions must the function satisfy?

Show model answer

Let ψ: Ω → R be (a) strictly convex, (b) continuously differentiable, (c) defined on a closed convex set Ω. Then Dψ(x, y) = ψ(x) − ψ(y) − ⟨ ∇ψ(y), x − y ⟩ for all x, y ∈ Ω.

It is the difference between ψ(x) and the first-order Taylor expansion of ψ around y evaluated at x. It represents the loss or distortion when y (the original point) is approximated by x (a noisy version).

Q15

List the properties of Bregman divergence.

Show model answer
  • Non-negativity: D(x, y) ≥ 0, and equals 0 iff x = y (from convexity).
  • Convexity in the first argument, but not necessarily in the second.
  • Linearity in the generating function: Daψ1 + bψ2 = a Dψ1 + b Dψ2 for a, b ≥ 0.
  • Not a metric: not symmetric in general and never satisfies the triangle inequality, but satisfies a generalised law of cosines and Pythagorean theorem.
  • Mean as minimiser: the mean vector minimises the expected Bregman divergence from a random vector.

Q17

What is Bregman information? Give examples.

Show model answer

For a random variable X with mean μ, the Bregman information is Iψ(X) = E[Dψ(X, μ)], the minimum expected distortion, achieved by the constant predictor μ. For squared loss the mean is the best constant predictor and the minimum loss is the variance.

  • ψ = ‖x‖²: variance.
  • ψ = negative entropy: mutual information I(Z; Y).
  • ψ = −Σ log xj, X uniform over points: log(AM/GM).
Q18

Explain edit distance. How is it computed?

Show model answer

The minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions and substitutions needed to convert one string into another. Dynamic programming: D(i, 0) = i, D(0, j) = j and D(i, j) = min{D(i−1, j) + 1, D(i, j−1) + 1, D(i−1, j−1) + [ai ≠ bj]}; the corner cell is the answer, and tracing back gives the operations. kitten → sitting = 3 (two substitutions and an insertion).

Q19

Explain Jaro and Jaro–Winkler similarity.

Show model answer

Jaro: simJ = 13(m/|s1| + m/|s2| + (m − t)/m), where m is the number of matching characters (equal and within ⌊ max(|s1|, |s2|)/2 ⌋ − 1 positions) and t is half the number of transpositions. Jaro–Winkler adds a prefix bonus: simW = simJ + ℓ p(1 − simJ) with ℓ the common prefix length (at most 4) and p = 0.1. It suits short strings such as names, because typos near the start are rarer.

Q20

What is an n-gram? How are n-grams used to compare two strings?

Show model answer

An n-gram is a continuous sequence of n items (letters, phonemes or words) from a text; n = 1 unigram, 2 bigram, 3 trigram. To compare strings, list their n-grams and compute the overlap: Dice = 2|G1 ∩ G2|/(|G1| + |G2|) or Jaccard = |G1 ∩ G2|/|G1 ∪ G2|. Night and nacht share only "ht": Dice = 2·1/8 = 0.25.

Q21

What is a language model? State the chain rule for a word sequence.

Show model answer

A language model assigns a probability to a sequence of words (how likely is this sentence?) or predicts the probability of the next word. Chain rule: P(w1 … wn) = P(w1)P(w2 | w1)P(w3 | w1, w2) ⋯ P(wn | w1 … wn−1) = Πi P(wi | w1 … wi−1). For long histories there is not enough data to estimate these terms, which motivates the Markov assumption.

Q22

Explain the Markov assumption and the relation between a Markov model and an N-gram model.

Show model answer

Instead of the full history, use only the previous k words: P(wi | w1 … wi−1) ≈ P(wi | wi−k … wi−1). This is a k-th order Markov model. An N-gram model uses only N − 1 words of prior context, so N-gram model = (N − 1)-order Markov model: unigram P(office), bigram P(office | from), trigram P(office | minutes from).

Q23

How are bigram probabilities computed from a corpus? What are <s> and </s>?

Show model answer

Using MLE, P(wn | wn−1) = C(wn−1, wn)/C(wn−1): the bigram count divided by the unigram count of the previous word. <s> and </s> are sentence-start and sentence-end markers; they capture sentence boundaries and word position, so the first word also has a context and the probabilities of all sentence lengths can be compared. Example: P(I | <s>) = 2/3 in the worked corpus.

Q24

What are the problems of the basic n-gram model? How are they solved?

Show model answer
  • Underflow from multiplying many small probabilities: work with logarithms and add them.
  • Zero probability for unseen bigrams: use smoothing, such as Laplace (add-one): P = (C(wi−1, wi) + 1)/(C(wi−1) + V) with V the number of unique words (excluding <s>).
  • Long-distance dependencies: n-grams only see N − 1 previous words, so they cannot capture agreement across long phrases; neural models address this.
  • Not enough data for long histories: use small n (bigram or trigram).
Q25

List applications of n-gram language models.

Show model answer
  • Search engines suggesting the next words while typing.
  • Speech recognition (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant) choosing the likelier transcription.
  • Machine translation (Google Translate) choosing the more fluent output.
  • Chatbots and predictive text on smartphones.
  • Spell-checking, plagiarism detection and text generation.

Q27

What is the curse of dimensionality and how does it affect similarity measures?

Show model answer

As the number of dimensions grows, data become sparse and the distances between pairs of points become nearly equal, so the nearest and farthest neighbours are hardly distinguishable and a distance loses its meaning. Remedies: dimensionality reduction or attribute subset selection, cosine similarity for sparse data, and choosing lower-order Minkowski distances (L1) in high dimensions.

Bigram model on the "Sam" corpus

Corpus: <s> I am Sam </s>, <s> Sam I am </s>, <s> I do not like green eggs and ham </s>. Find P(I | <s>), P(Sam | am), P(am | I), P(do | I) and the probability of the first sentence.

Show solution
  1. C(<s>) = 3, C(I) = 3, C(am) = 2, C(Sam) = 2.
  2. P(I | <s>) = 2/3 = 0.667.
  3. P(Sam | am) = 1/2 = 0.500.
  4. P(am | I) = 2/3 = 0.667; P(do | I) = 1/3 = 0.333.
  5. P(<s> I am Sam </s>) = P(I | <s>) P(am | I) P(Sam | am) P(</s> | Sam) = 0.667 × 0.667 × 0.500 × 0.500 = 0.1111.
P(I | <s>) = 2/3, P(Sam | am) = 1/2, P(am | I) = 2/3, P(do | I) = 1/3; sentence probability = 0.1111 (= 1/9).
P27

Underflow and logarithms

A 20-word sentence has an average word probability of 0.001. Why is the product a problem on a computer and what is the log-probability?

Show solution
  1. Product = 0.001²⁰ = 10⁻⁶⁰, which is tiny, and for a few hundred words it falls below the smallest representable floating-point number (underflow) and becomes 0.
  2. Use logs: log₁₀ P = Σ log₁₀ pi = 20 × (−3) = −60.
  3. Sums of logs are stable; to compare sentences, compare the log-probabilities (higher is more likely). Example from the smoothed sentence above: log₁₀(2.667e-05) = -4.574.
Always add log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities.
