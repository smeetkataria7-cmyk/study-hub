1. Unit 1.1: Data and attributes

Q1What is a data object and attributes? 5M

Data set is made up of data objects. A data object represents an entity. Examples: in a sales database the objects are customers, store items and sales; in a medical database they are patients, medicines and treatments.

Objects are described by attributes. An attribute represents a characteristic of the object, for example customer_ID, name, address. The set of attributes used to describe an object is called the attribute vector.

In a database, rows are data objects and columns are attributes. Other names for an attribute: variable (statistics), dimension (warehousing), feature (machine learning). Other names for an object: sample, instance, record, tuple, point, observation.

Q2Define data, information, science and data science. p1
  • Data: raw facts or figures that make no sense or have no meaning by themselves (numbers, dates, images, sound, video). It is raw information that is collected or stored, and can be structured (databases) or unstructured (text, images, video).
  • Information: data that has been processed by a computer to give it meaning. Example: 7, 7.2, 6 becomes "A 7, B 7.2, C 6" (student pointers).
  • Knowledge: information used to form judgements or decisions. Example: students with pointer above 7 are eligible for placement. added from slides
  • Science: systematic study of phenomena using scientific methods and principles.
  • Data science: an interdisciplinary field that combines statistics, machine learning and domain knowledge to extract insight and knowledge from structured and unstructured data.

2. Unit 1.2: Types of datasets

Q8Explain the general characteristics of datasets: dimensionality, distribution and resolution. p7–8
  • Dimensionality: the number of attributes the objects have. If there are a high number of attributes (high dimensionality) the data becomes difficult to analyse. This problem is the curse of dimensionality: data turns sparse, classification models lack enough objects to be reliable and clustering distances lose meaning. Remedy: dimensionality reduction.
  • Distribution: frequency of occurrence of various values or sets of values of the attributes comprising the objects. It describes the concentration of objects in various regions of the data space. A Gaussian distribution yields powerful analysis techniques. Sparsity means most values are zero; only the non-zero values need storing. added from slides
  • Resolution: patterns in the data depend on the level of resolution. Too fine and a pattern may not be visible or may be buried in noise (example: the earth's surface looks uneven at metre scale). Too coarse and the pattern may disappear (storms visible over hours are invisible over months).
Q9Explain the types of datasets. p8

Your notes list: numerical, bivariate, multivariate, categorical, correlation datasets, and record data (data matrix, document data, transaction data). Details added from slides:

  • Numerical: values are numbers, arithmetic possible (weight, height, RBC count).
  • Bivariate: two variables, studies their relationship (ice-cream sales vs temperature). One variable alone is univariate.
  • Multivariate: three or more variables (length, width, height, volume of a box).
  • Categorical: qualitative variables; dichotomous (two values, e.g. gender) or polytomous (more than two).
  • Correlation datasets: values related to each other, with positive, negative or zero correlation.
  • Record data: a collection of records, each with a fixed set of attributes and no explicit relationship between records. Data matrix: m objects by n numeric attributes. Document data: each document is a term vector (rows documents, columns terms, value = count). Transaction data: market-basket data, each record is a set of items bought in one trip.
  • Graph-based: objects as nodes and relationships as links (web pages, social networks), or objects that are graphs (chemical structures).
  • Ordered: sequence, time series, spatial and spatio-temporal data.

Q13Explain the applications of data science in healthcare. p5–7

Data science combines statistics, machine learning and domain knowledge to extract insight from data. In healthcare it plays a transformative role by analysing clinical, genomic and operational data from EHRs, medical imaging and wearable devices to improve diagnosis and treatment.

Key applications

  1. Predictive diagnosis: ML models (logistic regression, random forest, SVM) analyse patient history to predict diseases like diabetes and cancer at an early stage.
  2. Medical imaging: deep learning (CNNs) detects tumours and abnormalities in X-rays, MRIs and CT scans, improving diagnostic accuracy.
  3. Drug discovery: predictive modelling speeds up identification of drug compounds, reducing clinical trial time and cost.
  4. Personalised medicine: genomic data analysis enables treatment plans tailored to an individual's genetic profile.
  5. Public health surveillance: big data analytics forecasts disease outbreaks (e.g. COVID-19 modelling), aiding policy and resource planning.

Benefits

Early accurate diagnosis; reduced healthcare cost through preventive care; personalised treatment plans; improved hospital resource management.

Examples

IBM Watson Health (oncology treatment recommendation); Google DeepMind (early detection of diabetic retinopathy and acute kidney injury); predictive COVID-19 models (outbreak forecasting and vaccine distribution planning).

Data privacy and security (compliance with HIPAA/GDPR); data quality and interoperability across healthcare systems; algorithm bias affecting fairness of diagnostics.

5. Unit 2.1: Central tendency, grouped data, SD and variance

Q14What are basic statistical descriptors of data? p8–9

General characteristics of quantitative data are described by four groups of measures:

  • Central tendency (averages): the tendency of data to concentrate at a certain value at the centre of the distribution.
  • Variation or dispersion: how the data vary about a measure of central tendency.
  • Skewness: data in a frequency distribution may fall into symmetrical or asymmetrical patterns; the direction and degree of asymmetry is skewness.
  • Kurtosis: polygons of frequency distributions exhibit flatness or peakedness.
Q15Explain frequency distribution: included class, rules for classes, Sturges' rule. p9
  • A frequency distribution lists each value (or class) with the number of times it occurs. When the upper and lower limits of a class are both included, it is an inclusive class. When the upper limit belongs to the next class, it is an exclusive class.
  • Rules for classes: clearly defined; every value included; mutually exclusive and non-overlapping; equal width; the number of classes neither too small nor too large (at least 5).
  • Sturges' rule: k = 1 + 3.322 log10 N, where N is the total frequency (number of observations), not the range.
  • Class width h ≈ range / k. Mid-point = (lower limit + upper limit)/2.
  • Class boundaries (to convert inclusive to exclusive): subtract d/2 from the lower limit and add d/2 to the upper limit, where d is the gap between the upper limit of one class and the lower limit of the next. For 10–19, 20–29, d = 1, so the boundaries are 9.5–19.5, 19.5–29.5. added from slides
Q16Explain the arithmetic mean, weighted mean and the step-deviation method. p29–31
Mean x̄ = (1/N) Σ fixi   (ungrouped: Σx / n; grouped: x is the class mid-point)

Step-deviation method. Calculating Σfx/N for large values of f and x is tedious. The arithmetic is eased by taking deviations from an arbitrary point A (usually a value near the middle of the distribution). Since Σfidi = Σfixi − AN, we get (1/N)Σfidi = x̄ − A, so

x̄ = A + (h/N) Σ fidi,   di = (xi − A)/h

A = arbitrary point, h = width of the class interval.

Weighted mean added from slides: x̄w = Σwx / Σw. Used when items are not equally important. It equals the simple mean when weights are equal, exceeds it when larger weights go to larger values, and falls below it when larger weights go to smaller values.

Worked examples from your notes

  • Marks 0–10 … 50–60 with f = 12, 18, 27, 20, 17, 6 (N = 100), mid-points 5…55: mean = 2800/100 = 28.

Merits of mean: rigidly defined, easy, based on all observations, stable under sampling. Demerits: distorted by extreme values, cannot be calculated with open-end classes, not for qualitative data. added from slides

Q17Explain the median for ungrouped, discrete and grouped data with an example. p10–14
  • Ungrouped: arrange in ascending order; middle value if n is odd, mean of the two middle values if n is even.
  • Discrete frequency distribution: find N/2 and read the x whose cumulative frequency is first greater than or equal to N/2.
  • Continuous (grouped): convert to exclusive classes with boundaries, find N/2, locate the median class (first cf ≥ N/2), then
    Median = l + (h/f)(N/2 − c)
    l = lower boundary of the median class, c = cf of the class before it, f = its frequency, h = its width.

  3. The classes have a gap of 0.01, so make them continuous (subtract and add 0.005): the median class is 4.505–6.005, with l = 4.505, c = 730, f = 900, h = 1.5.
  4. Median = 4.505 + (1500 − 730)/900 × 1.5 = 4.505 + 1.283 = 5.79 hours.

Q18Explain the mode, the method of grouping and the grouped mode formula. p15–16
  • Mode is the value that occurs most often. A distribution may have one mode (unimodal), two (bimodal), three (trimodal) or none.
  • Method of grouping is used when the maximum frequency is repeated, falls at the very start or end, or the data is irregular. Steps:
    1. Column 1: the given frequencies (note the maximum).
    2. Column 2: add the frequencies in pairs from the start.
    3. Column 3: add in pairs, leaving the first one. (Leaving the first two just repeats column 2, so skip it, as your notes say.)
    4. Column 4: add in threes from the start.
    5. Column 5: add in threes, leaving the first one.
    6. Column 6: add in threes, leaving the first two.
    7. Mark the maximum in each column and note the x values that make it. The x value that appears in the most columns is the mode.
    In your example (size 1–12), the maximum of each column is 45 (x = 10), 75 (5, 6), 72 (6, 7), 98 (4, 5, 6), 107 (5, 6, 7), 100 (6, 7, 8). The value 6 appears in five columns, so mode = 6.
  • Grouped data formula:
    Mode = l + h(f1 − f0) / (2f1 − f0 − f2)
    f1 = frequency of the modal class, f0 = of the class before, f2 = of the class after. Example: classes 0–10 … 70–80, f = 5, 8, 7, 12, 28, 20, 10, 10. Modal class 40–50, l = 40, h = 10, f0 = 12, f1 = 28, f2 = 20. Mode = 40 + 10(16)/(56 − 12 − 20) = 40 + 160/24 = 46.67.
Q19Explain mean, mode, median and their relationship. p17
  • Mean: sum of all observations divided by their number. Median: middle value of the ordered data. Mode: most frequent value.
  • Symmetric distribution: mean = median = mode (all at the centre of the bell curve).
  • Positively skewed: long tail on the right; the mode occurs at a value smaller than the median; Mode < Median < Mean.
  • Negatively skewed: long tail on the left; the mode occurs at a value greater than the median; Mean < Median < Mode.
  • Empirical relationship (moderately skewed data):
    2 Mean + Mode = 3 Median  ⇒  Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean

Q20Explain the geometric mean with formula, merits, demerits and uses. p17

The geometric mean of n observations x1, …, xn is the n-th root of their product:

G = (x1·x2···xn)1/n  ⇒  log G = (1/n) Σ log xi  ⇒  G = antilog[(1/n) Σ log xi]

For a frequency distribution: G = antilog[Σ fi log xi / N]. Log G is the arithmetic mean of the logarithms.

  • Merits: rigidly defined, uses all values, suited to algebra, gives more weight to small items.
  • Demerits: hard for a non-mathematician; zero if any value is zero; imaginary if any value is negative. added from slides
  • Uses: rate of population growth, rate of interest, index numbers.

Example: GM of 3, 9, 27 = (729)1/3 = 9.

Q22Explain variance and standard deviation with the steps and an example. p20, p21
σ² = Σ(xi − x̄)² / N    σ = √[(1/N) Σ fi(xi − x̄)²]

Low standard deviations indicate data clustered near the measure of central tendency; high standard deviations indicate data spread out from the centre.

  1. Find the mean x̄ of the data.
  2. Find the difference between the mean and each data value and square it.
  3. Find the variance σ² by adding the squares of all the differences and dividing by the number of data values.
  4. Find the SD by taking the square root of the variance.

Example (elevator stops: 0, 3, 1, 1, 0, 5, 1, 0, 3, 0): mean = 14/10 = 1.4; squared differences 1.96, 2.56, 0.16, 0.16, 1.96, 12.96, 0.16, 1.96, 2.56, 1.96, sum = 26.4; variance = 2.64; SD = 1.62.

6. Unit 2.2: Dispersion, mean deviation, correlation

Q23Why do we need measures of dispersion? List them and the characteristics of a good measure. p18

Averages give an idea of the concentration of observations around the central part of the distribution, but averages alone cannot describe the data: different data sets can share the same mean yet differ widely in spread.

Characteristics of a good measure: rigidly defined; easy to calculate and understand; based on all observations; suited to further mathematical treatment; little affected by sampling fluctuations. last three added from slides

Measures: range, quartile (and interquartile range), variance, standard deviation (plus mean deviation).

Q24Define quantile, quartile, interquartile range. Explain the five-number summary and the boxplot with an example. p18–19
  • Quantiles: data points taken at regular intervals of the data distribution. 2-quantile = median; 4-quantiles = quartiles Q1, Q2, Q3; 100-quantiles = percentiles. Q1 is the 25th percentile, Q3 the 75th. added from slides
  • IQR = Q3 − Q1, the range covered by the middle half of the data.
  • Five-number summary: min, Q1, median, Q3, max.
  • Boxplot: the ends of the box are at the first and third quartiles, so the box length is the IQR; the median is a line inside the box; whiskers are two lines outside the box extending to the minimum and maximum (non-outlier) values; outliers are plotted individually.
  • Outliers added from slides: mild outliers lie beyond Q1 − 1.5·IQR or Q3 + 1.5·IQR (marked with a circle); extreme outliers lie beyond Q1 − 3·IQR or Q3 + 3·IQR (marked with an asterisk).

Example (the 12 salaries in Q21): Q1 = 47 and Q3 = 63 in your notes, so IQR = 16. You first computed Q1 = 48.5 and Q3 = 66.5 (median of each half) and then crossed that out.

Q27What is correlation? Explain scatter diagram, Karl Pearson's coefficient and Spearman's rank correlation. 10M, p34–36

Correlation studies the extent of the relationship between two or more variables. It measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship. Correlation does not imply causation. last line added from slides

  1. Scatter diagram: plot one variable on the x-axis and the other on the y-axis. Points on a narrow strip rising left to right: high positive correlation; falling: high negative; scattered all over: none.
  2. Karl Pearson's coefficient (r):
    r = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / √[Σ(x − x̄)² Σ(y − ȳ)²] = Cov(x, y)/(σxσy)
    r = [nΣxy − ΣxΣy] / √{[nΣx² − (Σx)²][nΣy² − (Σy)²]} = [Σxy − n x̄ȳ] / √[(Σx² − n x̄²)(Σy² − nȳ²)]
    x̄ and ȳ are the averages of x and y.
  3. Spearman's rank correlation (ρ): based on ranks. added from slides
    ρ = 1 − 6Σd² / [n(n² − 1)], d = rank of x − rank of y
    Tied values take the average rank, and each group of m tied values adds m(m² − 1)/12 to Σd².

Properties: −1 ≤ r ≤ +1; r = +1 perfect positive; r = −1 perfect negative; r = 0 no linear correlation. added from slides

Q29What is skewness? Explain Karl Pearson's and Bowley's coefficients. added from slides

Skewness is lack of symmetry: the direction and degree of asymmetry of a frequency distribution. Positive skew: long right tail, Mode < Median < Mean. Negative skew: long left tail, Mean < Median < Mode.

Karl Pearson: SkP = (Mean − Mode)/S.D.   or, if the mode is ill-defined, 3(Mean − Median)/S.D.
Bowley: SkB = (Q3 + Q1 − 2M)/(Q3 − Q1),   M = median, between −1 and +1

Pearson's absolute measure (mean − mode) cannot compare series in different units, so it is divided by σ. Bowley's uses only quartiles, so extreme values do not affect it and it works with open-end classes. Both are 0 for a symmetric distribution.

Example: Q1 = 40, M = 60, Q3 = 80 gives SkB = (80 + 40 − 120)/40 = 0, so the distribution is symmetric.

Q30What is kurtosis? Explain its types. added from slides

Kurtosis (Karl Pearson: "the amount of hump") is the peakedness or flatness of a frequency curve; the fourth central moment. It measures how much of the data sits in the tails rather than the centre. The normal curve is the yardstick.

Q31Explain relative frequency and the types of graphs for frequency distributions. p22, p27–28
  • Relative frequency = frequency/N (× 100 for relative percentage frequency). Example: 20 students, hours per day 2…7 with f = 3, 5, 3, 6, 2, 1 → relative % = 15, 25, 15, 30, 10, 5.
  • Histogram: summarises a univariate data set; adjacent bars on class boundaries. For ungrouped data with jump h between values, each bar spans x − h/2 to x + h/2 (e.g. h = 5, so ±2.5). Grouped, non-continuous data must first be made continuous.
  • Frequency polygon: plot (x or mid-point, f) and join the points by straight lines, extended to the axis at a hypothetical class before the first and after the last (frequency 0). It can also be drawn by joining the mid-points of the tops of the histogram bars.
  • Frequency curve: join the plotted points with a smooth freehand line (unlike the straight segments of a polygon).
  • Steps to make a frequency curve: (1) compute class mid-points, m = (lower + upper)/2; (2) add one hypothetical class before the first and one after the last, each with frequency 0; (3) plot the mid-points on the x-axis and the frequencies on the y-axis; (4) join with a smooth curve.
Q32Explain the cumulative frequency curve (ogive), less-than and more-than. p23–25
  • Less-than cf: running total from the first class (41, 92, 156, 194, 201 for wages 0–20 … 80–100). More-than cf: running total from the last class upward (201, 160, 109, 45, 7).
  • Plot less-than cf against the upper class limit (rises, S-shaped) and more-than cf against the lower limit (falls). The two curves cross at the median.
  • Median from the ogive: draw a horizontal line at N/2 on the cf axis to the curve, then read the value on the x-axis.

Example (ages of 700 students, page 24): N/2 = 350 lies between the cf values 342 (at age 9) and 438 (at age 10). By formula, median = 9 + (350 − 342)/96 = 9.08 years.

Q33Linear regression: find the line and predict. p37–38; regression is Lecture 13, not in the syllabus
