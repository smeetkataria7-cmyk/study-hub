1. Formula sheet

Symbols: n = number of observations, N = Σf, x = value (class mid-point for grouped data), f = frequency, h = class width, L = lower boundary of the class, cf = cumulative frequency of the class before.

Sturges: k = 1 + 3.322 log10 N    Class width h ≈ Range / k    Mid-point = (lower + upper)/2

F. Covariance and correlation

Cov(X, Y) = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)/n = Σxy/n − x̄ȳPositive: move together. Negative: move opposite. Zero: no linear relation. Units are (unit of X) × (unit of Y).
Pearson: r = Cov(X, Y)/(σxσy) = nΣxy − ΣxΣy√[(nΣx² − (Σx)²)(nΣy² − (Σy)²)]
Shortcut: use u = x − A, v = y − B in the same formula. r does not change when you shift the origin.
Spearman: ρ = 1 − 6Σd²n(n² − 1) (d = rankX − rankY)
Ties: add m(m² − 1)/12 to Σd² for every group of m tied values.
Concurrent deviations: rc = ± √(± (2c − m)/m)c = number of pairs of changes with the same sign, m = number of pairs of changes (n − 1). Take the sign of (2c − m) both inside and outside the root.
r lies in [−1, +1]  |  r² = coefficient of determination  |  1 − r² = coefficient of non-determination
Correlation does not imply causation.

2. Notes: Module 1, Fundamentals of data

1.1 Data, information, knowledge, attributes

TermMeaningExample from the slides

Data science is an interdisciplinary field that uses scientific methods, statistics, algorithms and domain expertise to extract knowledge and insight from structured data (databases) and unstructured data (text, images, video).

Data object: an entity described by attributes (a patient, a customer, a student). Other names: sample, instance, record, tuple, point, vector, pattern, event, case, observation. Database rows are objects and columns are attributes.

Attribute: a characteristic of an object. Other names: variable (statistics), dimension (warehousing), feature (machine learning), attribute (databases, data mining). A set of attributes describing one object is its attribute (feature) vector. One attribute gives a univariate distribution and two give a bivariate one.

Attribute versus attribute value. The same attribute can have different value sets (height in feet or metres). Different attributes can share a value set (ID and age are both integers) while having different properties (age supports averaging, ID does not).

Qualitative vs quantitative. Qualitative data is descriptive and categorical (words, images, audio) and answers why and how; arithmetic cannot be done on it. Quantitative data is numeric and measurable and answers how many, how much and how often; it supports statistical analysis.

1.2 Types of datasets and their characteristics

General characteristics

  • Dimensionality: the number of attributes of the objects. Curse of dimensionality: as dimensions grow the data becomes sparse in the space it occupies. For classification there are too few objects to build a reliable model; for clustering, density and distance become less meaningful. The remedy is dimensionality reduction.
  • Distribution: how often values or sets of values occur. Sparsity: most attribute values are 0 (often under 1% non-zero). Sparse data is cheap to store (keep only the non-zero entries) and is useful in association-rule mining, but skewed distributions make classification harder.
  • Resolution: the scale at which data is observed. Too fine and a pattern is buried in noise; too coarse and it disappears. Example: atmospheric pressure over hours shows storms, over months it does not.

Dataset by variables (Lecture 02 also classes them this way): numerical (quantitative), bivariate (two variables, e.g. ice-cream sales vs temperature), multivariate (three or more), categorical (dichotomous with two values, polytomous with more) and correlation datasets (positive, negative, zero).

3. Notes: Module 2, Exploring data using descriptive measures

2.0 Frequency distributions and graphs (the base for grouped data)

  • Frequency distribution: for each value (variable x) the number of times (frequency f) it occurs. For large data, group values into class intervals.
  • Classes should be clearly defined, mutually exclusive, non-overlapping, preferably equal width, and cover every value. Number of classes is not less than 5; Sturges: k = 1 + 3.322 log10N. Class width ≈ range / k. Choose limits so mid-points are round numbers (0, 2, 5, 10).
  • Inclusive classes (10–19, 20–29): both limits belong to the class. They suit discrete data. Exclusive classes (10–20, 20–30): the upper limit belongs to the next class. They suit continuous data and are needed for the median, quartile and histogram formulas.
  • To convert inclusive to exclusive, subtract d/2 from each lower limit and add d/2 to each upper limit. The new limits are the class boundaries.
  • Histogram: adjacent rectangles on class boundaries with height proportional to frequency (for unequal widths use frequency ÷ width). For an ungrouped distribution assume each value spreads over x − h/2 to x + h/2.
  • Frequency polygon: plot (mid-point, frequency) and join by straight lines, or join the mid-points of the tops of the histogram bars. Extend it to the axis at two hypothetical classes (before the first and after the last) with zero frequency. Frequency curve: a smooth freehand curve through the polygon's vertices.

2.1 Measures of central tendency

A measure of central tendency is a single value describing where most values lie. An ideal one is rigidly defined, easy to understand and calculate, based on all observations, suited to further mathematical treatment, little affected by sampling fluctuations and by extreme values.

MeasureMeritsDemeritsUse it when
Arithmetic meanRigidly defined, easy, uses all observations, stable under samplingPulled by outliers, cannot be found if a value is missing or the end class is open, not for qualitative data, misleading for very skewed dataSymmetric quantitative data
Weighted meanRespects importance of itemsNeeds sensible weightsItems matter unequally (marks with credit weights). Equals the simple mean when weights are equal. It exceeds the simple mean when larger values carry larger weights.
MedianNot affected by extremes, works with open-ended classes, only average usable for rankable qualitative dataNot based on all values, not suited to algebra, varies more across samplesSkewed data: wages, wealth
ModeEasy, unaffected by extremes, works for open classes and nominal dataMay be ill-defined or multiple (bimodal, multimodal) or absent, ignores most valuesMost common or "ideal size" (shoe and garment sizes)
Geometric meanRigidly defined, uses all values, gives more weight to small itemsZero if any value is zero, undefined for negatives, hard for non-mathematiciansGrowth rates, interest, index numbers

Trimmed mean: drop a small percentage (e.g. 2%) of the highest and lowest values before averaging. A trim as large as 20% loses useful information, and it is not suitable for skewed data.

Mode of a grouped distribution by formula needs a clear modal class. When the maximum frequency repeats, sits at the very start or end, or the data is irregular, use the method of grouping.

Relation: in a symmetric distribution mean = median = mode. In a moderately skewed one, Mean − Mode = 3(Mean − Median).

2.2 Measures of dispersion, covariance and correlation

Averages alone are inadequate. The sets 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 1, 5, 9, 13, 17 share a mean of 9 yet differ greatly in spread. Dispersion measures how scattered the values are. An ideal measure is rigidly defined, easy, based on all observations, suited to algebra and little affected by sampling.

FamilyMeasures
Distance between selected valuesRange, interquartile range (quartile deviation)
Average deviation from a central valueMean deviation, standard deviation, variance, root mean square deviation
Relative (unit-free) coefficientsCoefficient of range, of QD, of MD, of SD, coefficient of variation
  • Range uses only two values. Quartiles split sorted data into four parts: Q1 (25th percentile), Q2 = median (50th), Q3 (75th). IQR is the spread of the middle half.
  • Mean deviation uses absolute deviations (signs ignored). It is smallest when taken about the median.
  • Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean; SD is its square root. A low SD means values cluster near the mean.
  • RMS deviation measures deviations from any chosen point A, not necessarily the mean. s² = σ² + d², so it is smallest, equal to σ, when A is the mean.
  • Coefficients of dispersion are pure numbers, so they compare variability across series with different units or averages. CV = (σ/x̄)×100; the series with smaller CV is more consistent.

Correlation and covariance

  • Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between variables. It does not imply causation. Example: hospitals and car thefts in a region correlate because both depend on population.
  • Types: positive, negative or none (direction); simple, partial or multiple (number of variables); linear or non-linear (graph shape).
  • Methods: scatter diagram, Karl Pearson's r, Spearman's rank correlation ρ, concurrent deviations.
  • Scatter diagram: points on a narrow strip rising left to right: high positive; falling: high negative; widely spread: none.
  • Pearson's r needs numeric data and a roughly linear relation. r near +1 is strong positive, near −1 strong negative, near 0 weak or none.
  • Spearman's ρ works on ranks, so it suits ordinal data or when actual values are unavailable. Tied values take the average of their ranks and need the tie correction.
  • Concurrent deviations use only the direction of change; it is the quickest and roughest.
  • Covariance shows whether two variables move together but depends on units, so its size is hard to interpret. Dividing by σxσy gives r. If correlation is 0 then covariance is 0. Your slide adds that the converse is not true, but with r = Cov/(σxσy) and non-zero SDs, Cov = 0 also gives r = 0. What is safe to write in an exam: zero correlation means no linear relationship, and a non-linear relationship can still exist.

2.3 Boxplot, outliers, skewness and kurtosis

  • Boxplot: the box runs from Q1 to Q3 (its length is the IQR), a line marks the median, whiskers extend to the smallest and largest non-outlier values, and outliers are plotted individually. It displays the five-number summary and the skew: a median off-centre in the box, or unequal whiskers, indicates skewness.
  • Outliers: beyond 1.5×IQR from the quartiles (mild, drawn as a circle) or beyond 3×IQR (extreme, drawn as an asterisk).
  • Skewness is lack of symmetry. Positive: long right tail, Mode < Median < Mean. Negative: long left tail, Mean < Median < Mode.
  • Karl Pearson's coefficient is relative, so it compares skewness across series. Use 3(Mean − Median)/σ when the mode is ill-defined. Bowley's coefficient uses only quartiles, so it is unaffected by extreme values and works with open-ended classes. Both are 0 for a symmetric distribution.
  • Kurtosis is the peakedness (fourth central moment). Compare β2 with 3, the normal curve's value: above 3 is leptokurtic (peaked, heavy tails, large outliers), 3 is mesokurtic, below 3 is platykurtic (flat, light tails).
  • Coefficients based on moments: the skewness coefficient γ1 = μ3/μ23/2 and kurtosis β2 = μ4/μ2².

5. Answers

Theory: model answers

T1 Data is raw facts without context (7, 7.2, 6). Information is data with structure and context so it has meaning (student A has pointer 7, B 7.2, C 6). Knowledge is information used for judgement or decisions (students with pointer above 7 are eligible for placement). Good information is accurate, complete and timely.
T2 An attribute is a characteristic of a data object (also variable, dimension, feature). Nominal: names without order (hair colour, zip code); only = and ≠. Ordinal: ordered but gaps unknown (grades, ranks); adds < and >. Interval: equal units, no true zero (°C, dates); adds + and −. Ratio: true zero (Kelvin, weight, counts, money); adds × and ÷. Typical averages: mode, median, mean, mean.
T3 Discrete attributes take countable values (number of students) and come from counting. Continuous attributes take any real value in a range (height) and come from measuring. Yes, a binary attribute is a special case of a discrete attribute.
T4 Symmetric binary: both outcomes are equally important (gender: male/female). Asymmetric binary: one outcome matters more (medical test: positive vs negative); by convention code 1 for the important outcome (e.g. HIV positive).
T5 Record: a collection of records with a fixed set of attributes and no explicit relationship between records (data matrix, document-term matrix, transaction/market-basket data). Graph-based: relations between objects held as links (web pages, social networks), or objects that are themselves graphs (chemical structure). Ordered: attributes have an order in time or space (sequence, time series, spatial and spatio-temporal data, sequential transactions).
T6 Dimensionality is the number of attributes. Curse of dimensionality: with many dimensions the data becomes sparse, classification models lack enough objects to be reliable and clustering distances lose meaning; reduce dimensions. Sparsity: most values are 0, so store only non-zero values (saves space, useful for association rules). Resolution: the scale of observation; too fine hides patterns in noise, too coarse removes them.
T7 Time series: measurements over time (daily stock prices) so temporal correlation matters. Sequence: ordered items with positions but no timestamps (DNA nucleotides, words). Spatial: position or area attributes (weather maps) with spatial correlation, where nearby places have similar values.
T8 Use the table in section 2 (1.3). In one line each: Data science is the broad field using ML and statistics for insight and prediction; data analytics analyses existing data to answer specific questions; data engineering builds the pipelines and storage that make data clean and accessible ("builds the roads").
T9 Use the EDA table in section 2 (1.3). Key points: sequence (classical imposes a model before analysis, EDA analyses first and lets the data suggest the model); focus (model vs data); techniques (quantitative tests vs graphics); rigour; and information loss (classical compresses data to a few estimates, EDA shows all data).
T10 Healthcare: outbreak and readmission prediction, medical imaging, personalised medicine. Agriculture: yield prediction, precision farming, pest and disease detection from imagery. Disaster management: forecasting floods and cyclones, damage assessment from satellite imagery, crisis mapping from social media, evacuation and resource planning. (Agriculture and disaster points are general knowledge, not from your slides.)
T11 Requisites: rigidly defined, easy to understand and calculate, based on all observations, suitable for further maths, little affected by sampling fluctuation or extreme values. Mean merits: rigidly defined, simple, uses all values, stable. Demerits: distorted by outliers, cannot be calculated with open-end classes or a missing value, unsuitable for qualitative data or very skewed data.
T12 The median is preferred for skewed data or outliers (wages, wealth) and for rankable qualitative data. Mode merits: easy, unaffected by extremes, usable with open classes and nominal data, gives the "typical" or ideal size. Demerits: may be absent, ill-defined or multiple, ignores most values, not suited to algebra, varies more with sampling.
T13 Symmetric: mean = median = mode. Positive skew: mode < median < mean. Negative skew: mean < median < mode. For moderate skew, mean − mode = 3(mean − median).
T14 Averages do not show spread; sets with the same mean can differ widely. Absolute measures (in data units): range, IQR/quartile deviation, mean deviation, standard deviation, variance. Relative measures (pure numbers): coefficients of range, QD, MD and SD, and the coefficient of variation.
T15 CV = (σ/x̄)×100 %. It is unit-free, so it compares the variability of series with different units or very different means. The series with the smaller CV is more consistent.
T16 A boxplot draws a box from Q1 to Q3 with the median marked inside and whiskers out to the extreme non-outlier values. The five-number summary is min, Q1, median, Q3, max. Points beyond Q1 − 1.5·IQR or Q3 + 1.5·IQR are mild outliers (circle); beyond 3·IQR they are extreme (asterisk).
T17 Skewness is lack of symmetry (direction and degree of asymmetry); kurtosis is the peakedness or tail weight relative to the normal curve. Kurtosis types: leptokurtic (β2 > 3), mesokurtic (= 3), platykurtic (< 3).
T18 Correlation measures the strength and direction of linear association and is unit-free, in [−1, 1]. Covariance measures joint variability, carries units and has no fixed range; r = Cov/(σxσy). Correlation does not imply causation: a third factor (population links hospitals and car thefts) may drive both.
T19 Pearson: numeric data, linear relation, uses actual values. Spearman: ranks, for ordinal data or when only rankings are known, handles ties with a correction. Concurrent deviations: only the direction of change in successive values; quickest and roughest, suited to time-ordered data and a rough indication.
T20 Inclusive classes (10–19) include both limits, suiting discrete data. Exclusive classes (10–20) put the upper limit in the next class, suiting continuous data. Convert inclusive to exclusive by subtracting d/2 from lower limits and adding d/2 to upper limits. Histogram: adjacent bars on class boundaries, height ∝ frequency. Polygon: plot (mid-point, f), join with lines, close to the axis at two zero-frequency classes. Curve: smooth freehand curve through the polygon.
