Descriptive statistics

Centre, spread, shape and relationships.
2.1

Descriptive statistics: purpose

Summarise a dataset with a few numbers: centre (central tendency), spread (dispersion), shape (skewness, kurtosis) and relationships (covariance, correlation). It describes the data at hand; it does not generalise beyond them.

2.2

Steps to build a frequency distribution

  • Find the range = max − min and choose the number of classes (Sturges' rule k = 1 + 3.322 log10 N).
  • Class width h = range / k, rounded; classes must be exclusive and of equal width.
  • Tally the observations into classes to get frequencies; add cumulative and relative frequencies.
  • Convert inclusive classes (10–19, 20–29) to continuous boundaries (9.5–19.5, …) before using grouped formulas.

2.4

Mean, median and mode together

In a symmetric distribution they coincide. In a positively skewed distribution Mean > Median > Mode (long right tail); in a negatively skewed one Mean < Median < Mode. For moderately skewed data Mode ≈ 3 Median − 2 Mean. The mean is the balance point; the median splits the data in half; the mode is the peak.

2.5

Measures of dispersion

MeasureMeaningMerits / demerits
RangeMax − minSimplest; depends on two values only
Quartile deviationHalf the spread of the middle 50%Ignores extremes; ignores the other 50%

Dispersion tells how reliable an average is: low spread means values cluster near the centre. Two datasets with the same mean can be very different in spread.

2.6

Quartiles, IQR and the boxplot

  • Quartiles Q1, Q2, Q3 split the ordered data into four equal parts; IQR = Q3 − Q1 is the spread of the middle half.
  • Five-number summary: min, Q1, median, Q3, max; the boxplot draws the box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median and whiskers to the most extreme non-outlier values.
  • Outliers lie beyond Q1 − 1.5,IQR or Q3 + 1.5,IQR (mild) and beyond 3 IQR (extreme).
  • Reading it: the median's position in the box and the whisker lengths show skewness; box length shows spread.
2.7

Skewness

Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution. Positive skew: long right tail, a few large values pull the mean up. Negative skew: long left tail. Zero: symmetric. Measures: Karl Pearson (uses mean, mode or median and σ), Bowley (uses quartiles, suits open classes) and the moment measure (β1, γ1).

2.8

Kurtosis

  • Kurtosis measures the peakedness and tail weight relative to the normal curve (β2 = 3).
  • Leptokurtic (β2 > 3): sharp peak, heavy tails, more outliers. Mesokurtic (β2 = 3): normal. Platykurtic (β2 < 3): flat top, light tails.
  • Excess kurtosis γ2 = β2 − 3.
2.9

Moments

The r-th central moment mr = Σ f(x − x̄)r / N summarises shape: m2 is the variance, m3 gives skewness and m4 kurtosis. Raw moments about an assumed mean A are easier to compute and are converted to central moments by formula.

2.10

Covariance and correlation

  • Covariance: how two variables vary together; positive (same direction), negative (opposite), zero (no linear relation). It depends on units.
  • Correlation coefficient r scales covariance to −1…+1: +1 perfect positive, −1 perfect negative, 0 none. It measures linear association only.
  • Correlation is not causation: a third variable or chance may explain the relationship.
  • Scatter-plot shapes: upward band (positive), downward band (negative), cloud (none), curve (non-linear, so r can be near 0 despite a strong relation).

2.12

Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

  • Tests whether the means of three or more groups differ, using one test instead of many pairwise t-tests (which inflate the error rate).
  • Splits total variation into between-group and within-group parts; the F ratio = MSB / MSW. A large F (above the table value) rejects H₀ that all means are equal.
  • Assumptions: independent observations, normally distributed groups, equal variances.
  • A significant result says some mean differs, not which one (post-hoc tests follow).
