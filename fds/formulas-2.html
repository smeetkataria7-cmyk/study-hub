2

Frequency tabulation

Build the table correctly and every later formula falls out of it.
2.1

Sturges’ rule — number of classes

k = 1 + 3.322 log10N

N = total frequency. Never fewer than five classes; they must be mutually exclusive, non-overlapping and of equal width.

2.2

Magnitude of the class interval

h = Rangek = xmax − xmink

Round to the nearest convenient integer. Wide intervals give rough estimates; narrow intervals give accuracy.

2.3

Class mid-value

m = lower limit + upper limit2

The mid-value stands in for x in every grouped-data formula on this sheet.

2.4

Inclusive → exclusive class boundaries

d = next lower limit − this upper limit upper boundary = upper limit + d2 lower boundary = lower limit − d2

For 10–19, 20–29 the gap is d = 1, so the boundaries become 9.5–19.5, 19.5–29.5 — each upper boundary now meets the next lower one.

2.5

Histogram from discrete (ungrouped) data

interval for value x :   x − h2  to  x + h2

h = the jump from one variate value to the next. With unequal class widths, rectangle height ∝ f / width, not f.

2.6

Frequency polygon & curve

  • Polygon — plot (mid-value, frequency) and join with straight lines: the midpoints of the tops of the histogram bars.
  • Curve — a smooth freehand curve through those same vertices.
  • Extend both ends down to the x-axis at the mid-values of two hypothetical classes, one before the first and one after the last, each with zero frequency.
3

Central tendency

A single value standing for the whole distribution.
3.1

Arithmetic mean

ungrouped   x̄ = Σxin grouped   x̄ = Σfi xiΣfi = Σf xN
3.2

Step-deviation (short-cut) method

di = xi − Ah  ⇒  x̄ = A + Σf dN × h

A = assumed mean, any convenient mid-value; h = class width. Cuts the arithmetic on wide-valued tables.

3.3

Weighted arithmetic mean

x̄w = Σwi xiΣwi

Equal weights give the simple mean. Large weights on large values push it above the simple mean; large weights on small values pull it below.

3.4

Median — ungrouped

n odd   M = n + 12th item n even   M = mean of the n2th and (n2+1)th items

Arrange the values in ascending order first.

3.5

Median — discrete frequency distribution

find N2,   N = Σfi

Take the “less than” cumulative frequency just greater than N/2 — the corresponding value of x is the median.

3.6

Median — continuous distribution

M = l + N2 − cf × h

l = lower boundary of the median class (first class whose c.f. exceeds N/2), c = c.f. of the class before it, f = its frequency, h = its width.

3.7

Mode — discrete

Z = value of x with the maximum frequency

One, two or three modes → unimodal, bimodal, trimodal. If the maximum frequency repeats, falls at either end, or the distribution is irregular, use the method of grouping.

3.8

Mode — continuous

Z = l + f1 − f02f1 − f0 − f2 × h = l + D1D1 + D2 × h

f1 = modal-class frequency, f0 and f2 the ones before and after; D1 = f1−f0, D2 = f1−f2.

3.9

Empirical relation

Mean − Median = 13(Mean − Mode) Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean

Use it to recover whichever of the three you were not given.

3.10

Geometric mean

G = (x1x2 … xn)1/n = antilog Σ log xin frequency data   G = antilog Σ fi log xiN

log G is the arithmetic mean of the logarithms. A zero in the data makes G = 0; a negative value makes it imaginary. Used for growth rates and index numbers.

3.11

Midrange

midrange = max + min2

A quick centre for numeric data, but determined entirely by the two extremes.

3.12

Trimmed mean

Drop the top and bottom 2% of values before averaging — this offsets a handful of extreme values, which the plain mean is highly sensitive to. Trimming as much as 20% throws away useful information, and it remains unsuitable for strongly skewed data.

3.13

Harmonic mean

HM = nΣ 1xifrequency data  HM = NΣ fixi

The reciprocal of the mean of reciprocals. Use it for averaging rates such as speed over equal distances. For positive, unequal data: HM < GM < AM.

3.14

Deciles & percentiles — position in ungrouped data

Dk :   k(n + 1)10th itemPk :   k(n + 1)100th itemQk :   k(n + 1)4th item

Sort ascending first. If the position is fractional, interpolate between the two neighbouring values. For grouped data replace n + 1 by N and use the median formula (3.6) with kN/10, kN/100 or kN/4.

4

Quantiles & the five-number summary

Position measures, and the fences that flag outliers.
4.1

The kth q-quantile

The value of x with at most k/q of the data below it and at most (q−k)/q above it, for integer 0 < k < q. There are q−1 of them.

  • 2-quantile → the median
  • 4-quantiles → quartiles Q1, Q2, Q3
  • 100-quantiles → percentiles
4.2

Quartiles — grouped data

Q1 = l + N4 − cf × h Q3 = l + 3N4 − cf × h

The same interpolation as the median, with N/4 and 3N/4 locating the class. Q1 is the 25th percentile, Q3 the 75th.

4.3

Outlier fences

inner   Q1 − 1.5 IQR    Q3 + 1.5 IQR outer   Q1 − 3 IQR    Q3 + 3 IQR

Past the inner fence but inside the outer → mild outlier, marked with a circle. Past the outer fence → extreme outlier, marked with an asterisk.

Box length = IQR = 15. The upper whisker stops at 52, the largest value inside the inner fence of 57.5; 70 sits beyond it and is plotted on its own.

5

Dispersion

Averages alone cannot separate 7, 8, 10, 11 from 1, 5, 9, 13, 17.
5.1

Range

R = max(x) − min(x)
5.2

Interquartile range & quartile deviation

IQR = Q3 − Q1     Q.D. = Q3 − Q12

The range covered by the middle half of the data.

5.3

Mean deviation about an average A

M.D. = Σ fi |xi − A|N

A may be the mean, median or mode, and signs are ignored. Mean deviation is least about the median, so deviations are normally taken from there.

5.4

Variance

σ2 = Σfi(xi − x̄)2N computational   σ2 = Σf x2N − x̄ 2

The average of the squared differences from the mean.

5.5

Standard deviation

σ = Σfi(xi − x̄)2N short-cut   σ = h Σf d2N − (Σf dN)2

Low σ means values cluster near the centre; high σ means they are spread out.

5.6

Root-mean-square deviation

s2 = Σfi(xi − A)2N = σ2 + d 2,   d = x̄ − A

So s is smallest, and equal to σ, when A is the mean: the mean minimises the r.m.s. deviation.

5.7

Population vs sample variance

population  σ2 = Σ (xi − μ)2Nsample  s2 = Σ (xi − x̄)2n − 1

Divide by n − 1 when the data are a sample used to estimate the variance of a larger population (Bessel's correction). Divide by N when you hold the whole population or a frequency table treated as the population, as in 5.4.

7

Moments

The engine behind both skewness and kurtosis.
7.1

Raw moments about an assumed mean

Mr = Σ f d rΣf,   d = x − Ah

Lay out columns for f, d, fd, fd2, fd3, fd4 and read M1…M4 straight off the totals.

7.2

Central moments

mr = Σ f(x − x̄)rN

Deviations from the mean raised to the power r. Always m1 = 0 and m2 = σ2.

7.3

Central from raw moments

m2 = (M2 − M12) h2 m3 = (M3 − 3M2M1 + 2M13) h3 m4 = (M4 − 4M3M1 + 6M2M12 − 3M14) h4

Drop the powers of h if the deviations were not divided by the class width.

8

Skewness

Lack of symmetry — both its direction and its degree.
8.1

Karl Pearson’s coefficient

Skp = Mean − ModeS.D. mode ill-defined   Skp = 3(Mean − Median)S.D.

The absolute measure (mean − mode) cannot compare series measured in different units, which is why it is divided by σ.

8.2

Bowley’s coefficient

SkB = Q3 + Q1 − 2MQ3 − Q1

Between −1 and +1; zero when Q1 and Q3 are equidistant from the median. The one to use with open-end classes.

8.3

Moment coefficient

β1 = m32m23     γ1 = m3m2m2

γ1 keeps the sign that squaring costs β1: positive means a longer right tail, negative a longer left tail, zero a symmetric curve.

9

Kurtosis

Peakedness — Karl Pearson’s “amount of hump”, the 4th central moment.
9.1

Coefficients

β2 = m4m22     excess   γ2 = β2 − 3

The normal curve is the yardstick, at β2 = 3 and γ2 = 0. It measures how much of the data sits in the tails rather than the centre.

  • Leptokurtic — β2 > 3. A high thin peak with heavy tails, so large outliers are likely.
  • Mesokurtic — β2 = 3. The normal curve, neither peaked nor flat.
  • Platykurtic — β2 < 3. Flat-topped with flat tails, so few extreme values.

10

Correlation

The extent of the relationship between two variables.
10.1

Covariance

cov(x, y) = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)N
10.2

Karl Pearson’s r

r = cov(x, y)σx σy = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)Σ(x − x̄)2 Σ(y − ȳ)2

Also called the product-moment correlation coefficient: the covariance divided by the product of the two standard deviations.

10.3

Computational (raw-score) form

r = NΣxy − Σx ΣyNΣx2 − (Σx)2 NΣy2 − (Σy)2

The workhorse in exams — five columns: x, y, x2, y2, xy.

10.4

Spearman’s rank correlation

no ties   ρ = 1 − 6 Σd2N(N2 − 1) with ties   ρ = 1 − 6 [ Σd2 + Σ m(m2 − 1)12 ]N(N2 − 1)

d = rank of x minus rank of y, and Σd must come to zero as a check. Tied values share the average rank, and one correction term is added for each group of m tied values.

10.5

Concurrent deviations

rc = ±±2C − NN

Only the direction of change matters. C = the number of concurrent deviations, where x and y move the same way; N = pairs of deviations compared, one fewer than the observations. If (2C−N) is negative, so is rc.

10.6

Properties of r

  • −1 ≤ r ≤ +1. Zero means no linear correlation; +1 is perfect positive, −1 perfect negative.
  • r2 = coefficient of determination.
  • K2 = 1 − r2 = coefficient of non-determination; K = √(1−r2) is the coefficient of alienation.
  • Standard error: S.E.(r) = (1 − r2) / √N.
10.7

Sample covariance & correlation matrix

sample  cov(x, y) = Σ (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ)n − 1cov(x, y) = Σ xyn − x̄ ȳ

Positive covariance: the variables move together. Negative: they move in opposite directions. Zero: no linear relation. Covariance depends on units; dividing by σx σy gives the unit-free r.

10.8

Regression lines (least squares)

y = a + bxb = N Σ xy − Σ x Σ yN Σ x2 − (Σ x)2 = cov(x, y)σx2a = ȳ − bx̄

The line that minimises the sum of squared vertical errors. It always passes through (x̄, ȳ). The regression coefficients satisfy byx, bxy = r2.

11

One-way ANOVA

Self-learning topic: are several group means the same?
11.1

Idea of one-way ANOVA

Tests whether the means of k groups are all equal, H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μ_k. It compares the variation between group means with the variation within groups. If between-group variation is large relative to within-group variation, the means differ.

Assumptions: independent samples, roughly normal groups, equal variances.

11.2

Sums of squares

SSB = Σ ni (x̄i − x̄)2SSW = Σ Σ (xij − x̄i)2SST = SSB + SSW = Σ (x − x̄)2

SSB = between groups (explained), SSW = within groups (error), SST = total. x̄ is the grand mean, x̄i the mean of group i.

11.3

Mean squares and the F statistic

MSB = SSBk − 1MSW = SSWN − kF = MSBMSW

k = number of groups, N = total observations. Reject H₀ if F exceeds the table value for (k − 1, N − k) degrees of freedom.
