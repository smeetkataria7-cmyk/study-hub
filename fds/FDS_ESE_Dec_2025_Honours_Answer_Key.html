Q1 (ii) Applications of Data Science in agriculture

  • Crop yield prediction: ML models use weather, soil and historical yield data to forecast production, helping planning and pricing.
  • Precision farming: sensors and drones measure soil moisture and nutrients, so water and fertiliser are applied only where needed.
  • Disease and pest detection: image classification (CNNs) on leaf photos identifies diseases early; apps like Plantix do this on a phone.
  • Weather forecasting and irrigation scheduling: forecasts decide when to sow, irrigate and harvest.
  • Soil health analysis: recommending crops and fertilisers from soil test data.
  • Market price prediction and supply chain: forecasting prices so farmers choose when and where to sell, and reducing wastage.
  • Livestock monitoring: wearable sensors track animal health and detect illness.

Q1 (iii) Time series data and a real-world application

Time series data is a sequence of observations of a variable recorded at successive, usually equally spaced, points in time: (t₁, x₁), (t₂, x₂), … The order matters, and nearby values are usually related.

Components:

  • Trend: long-term upward or downward movement.
  • Seasonality: regular patterns that repeat over a fixed period (daily, monthly, yearly).
  • Cyclic variation: longer, irregular-length rises and falls (business cycles).
  • Irregular (random) variation: unpredictable noise.

Application, stock market forecasting: daily closing prices of a share form a time series. Analysts plot it, compute moving averages to smooth noise, and fit models such as ARIMA or LSTM to forecast future prices and decide when to buy or sell.

Other applications: electricity demand forecasting, weather forecasting, ECG monitoring, monthly sales forecasting.

Q3 (ii) Normalising age = 35

27 values, min = 13, max = 70, mean = 809/27 = 29.96, σ = 12.94 (given).

(a) Min-max normalisation to [0.0, 1.0]

v′ = (v − min)/(max − min) × (new_max − new_min) + new_min = (35 − 13)/(70 − 13) = 22/57 = 0.386

(b) z-score normalisation

v′ = (v − mean)/σ = (35 − 29.96)/12.94 = 5.04/12.94 = 0.389 ≈ 0.39

(c) Decimal scaling

v′ = v/10ʲ, where j is the smallest integer with max|v′| < 1. Max |v| = 70, so j = 2.
v′ = 35/100 = 0.35
Min-max 0.386, z-score 0.39, decimal scaling 0.35

(d) Which method to prefer: the data contains an outlier (70), far from the rest (most ages are 13–46). Min-max uses the max, so the outlier squeezes all other values into a small range near 0. Decimal scaling is also driven by the largest value. z-score normalisation is preferred because it uses the mean and standard deviation, which are less affected by a single extreme value, and it keeps values comparable across attributes. (Min-max is acceptable only if the fixed range [0, 1] is required.)

Q5: Graphs and charts for visualising complex datasets (10 marks)

Data visualisation is the graphical representation of data to reveal patterns, trends, outliers and relationships. Complex datasets (many variables, many records, hierarchies, networks, time) need more than simple charts.

  1. Histogram: Distribution of one numeric variable; bars for frequencies of class intervals. Example: distribution of exam marks.
  2. Box plot (box-and-whisker): Five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) and outliers; compares distributions across groups. Example: salaries by department.
  3. Scatter plot: Relationship between two numeric variables; shows correlation and clusters. Example: height vs weight.
  4. Scatter-plot matrix: Grid of scatter plots for every pair of attributes; used for multi-dimensional data such as the Iris dataset.
  5. Heat map: Matrix whose cells are coloured by value. Example: correlation matrix of features, or website click density.
  6. Line chart: Trend over time for time series. Example: daily stock prices or monthly sales.
  7. Bar / stacked bar chart: Compare categories; stacked bars show parts of a whole within each category. Example: sales by region split by product.
  8. Pie / donut chart: Proportions of a whole. Example: market share. Use only for a few categories.
  9. Bubble chart: Scatter plot with a third variable as bubble size (and a fourth as colour). Example: GDP vs life expectancy, bubble = population.
  10. Parallel coordinates: Each attribute is a vertical axis; each record is a polyline across the axes. Shows patterns in high-dimensional data.
  11. Tree map: Nested rectangles sized by value for hierarchical data. Example: disk usage by folder, or stock market sectors.
  12. Network (graph) diagram: Nodes and edges for relationships. Example: social network of friends.
  13. Geographic map (choropleth): Regions shaded by value. Example: COVID cases by state.
  14. Word cloud / tag cloud: Text data; word size shows frequency. Example: most common words in customer reviews.
  15. Chernoff faces and stick figures: Icon-based methods for multi-dimensional data, mapping attributes to facial features or limb angles.

Choosing a chart: distribution → histogram or box plot; relationship → scatter plot or heat map; comparison → bar chart; composition → pie chart or tree map; trend → line chart; high dimensions → parallel coordinates or scatter-plot matrix; network → graph diagram.
